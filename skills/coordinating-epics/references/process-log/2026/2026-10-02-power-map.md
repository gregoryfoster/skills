### Findings that changed the protocol

Numbers are the comment's own (54–63). This comment started at 54 because two earlier comments on #348 both used 38.

**Creating the epic**

- 54: after an architecture change, creation gives each issue one of four outcomes: keep; rework, with the new scope on the line; close, proposed and pending approval; or park, waiting on a named trigger.
- 55: measure the shared premise once and put it at the head of the epic, in a "What the cutover changed (measured `<date>`)" block. A read-only request-log query showed nothing had written to the observation endpoint since the cutover. Lines cite the block, and so did the follow-ups the CannObserv/power-map#481 thread filed.
- 56: the operator's first closures set a classification rule. A correctness bug on a surface being kept stays open even when nothing uses it today. An enhancement on an unused surface closes with a reopen trigger or shrinks to a docs-only "decided: no". Three later follow-ups were classified against it without asking.
- 57: closed issues can leave steps that never landed. A systemd unit still held a temporary retention value with a comment pointing at a closed issue, and a deferred retirement lived only in host memory. At creation, grep live config for `TEMPORARY`, `revert` and refs to closed issues, and read host memory for scope that never reached GitHub.

**Closing in triage**

- 58: walk proposed closures one at a time in four parts: what the issue asks, what changed since with evidence, the recommendation, and the one reason to keep it. The fourth part made each decision quick.
- 59: every "not planned" close ends with "When to reopen: …", naming the condition and, where the issue's own fix would be wrong by then, the cheaper option to weigh instead.

**Writing to a public repo**

- 60: draft the epic body to a file first, so a refused create costs one handed-over command. On a public repo, state the conclusion ("no writers since `<date>`") and keep the per-client breakdown in the coordinator's notes.

**Check-ins**

- 61: a scope item that says "check the sibling surfaces for the same shape" generates follow-ups, and the brief should warn that the epic will grow. Slotting the four: small bugs went ahead of a docs-only item already queued, and among them the admin hard-delete on a surface in daily use went ahead of a 500 on an endpoint nobody calls.
- 62: offer to bundle as well as to split. Two pairs of follow-ups share a file and a family, so their lines say "can share a thread with #N". Each issue otherwise pays a full ship cycle, and the operator decides.
- 63: at each check-in, re-check the loose ends the operator owns by searching the upstream repo by subject keywords, and rewrite the line when one has resolved.

### What v1's scripts would and wouldn't have caught

- **Would:** finding 61's arrivals. `scan.sh`'s number watermark lists the four follow-ups and the FYI whether or not they mention the epic. Finding 55's block has its own op, `replace-section`.
- **Would, after the hand-filed create:** `epic-edit.sh --adopt-live` takes the operator's body as the first baseline, and every later edit is diffed against it and read back.
- **Wouldn't:** finding 60. `epic-edit.sh` edits an existing epic and never creates one, and its lint screens for secrets and placeholders, not for production detail unfit for a public repo.
- **Wouldn't:** findings 57 and 63. The drafted upstream issue had no ref in the body, so `scan.sh`'s cross-repo states couldn't see it, and neither script searches another repo by keyword or greps config and host memory.
